\section{Conclusion}

This research develops a reproducible framework for studying whether next-month U.S. stock-return predictability varies across volatility regimes. The production pipeline combines CRSP monthly returns, Compustat accounting data linked through CCM, and VIX; constructs log market equity, book-to-market, and momentum 12--2 under explicit information-timing restrictions; and estimates Elastic Net and XGBoost using a strictly time-respecting expanding-window design.

The production out-of-sample evaluation contains 893,486 stock-month predictions across 311 formation months from January 2000 through November 2025. Return-level forecast accuracy is weak in an unconditional sense: overall OOS $R^2$ is approximately zero for both models relative to the pooled expanding historical-mean benchmark.

The cross-sectional evidence is more informative. XGBoost's mean monthly Spearman information coefficient is 0.0187 in LOW-VIX months and is statistically positive under Newey--West/HAC inference ($p=0.013$). Its HIGH-VIX rank IC is approximately zero. Elastic Net does not produce statistically significant mean rank IC in either regime.

Economic-value tests show the same concentration. The XGBoost equal-weighted LOW-VIX $D10-D1$ portfolio earns 0.969\% per month gross and 0.748\% per month after the frozen 50-basis-point one-way turnover-cost specification. The net LOW-VIX estimate has HAC $t=2.82$ and $p=0.0049$. The signal disappears under value weighting, indicating that the baseline economic effect is concentrated away from the largest stocks.

The regime interpretation must nevertheless remain cautious. The direct LOW-minus-HIGH XGBoost rank-IC difference has $p=0.105$, and the direct equal-weighted net portfolio difference has $p=0.128$. The evidence therefore supports the statement that predictive content is concentrated in LOW-VIX states, but it does not establish a conventionally significant structural difference between LOW- and HIGH-VIX regimes.

The baseline findings motivate several next steps. Temporal stability should be evaluated formally, alternative volatility-regime definitions should be compared, richer characteristic sets can test whether the result survives a broader information environment, and transaction-cost assumptions can be strengthened for smaller stocks. These extensions will determine whether the LOW-VIX nonlinear signal is robust enough to support a broader claim of volatility-dependent stock-return predictability.
