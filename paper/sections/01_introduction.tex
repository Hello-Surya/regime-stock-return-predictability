\section{Introduction}

The predictability of stock returns need not be constant across market environments. Periods of elevated market volatility can coincide with changes in risk premia, investor behavior, information processing, and the cross-sectional dispersion of returns. This research therefore asks whether next-month stock-return predictability differs between high- and low-volatility regimes and whether a nonlinear machine-learning model captures regime-dependent predictability differently from a regularized linear model.

The baseline empirical design compares Elastic Net and XGBoost using the same information set and the same strictly out-of-sample evaluation structure. At prediction month $t$, all characteristics and the volatility regime are constructed using information available through $t$, while the prediction target is the realized stock return in calendar month $t+1$. Models are estimated only on historical observations whose outcomes would already have been observed. Performance is then evaluated overall and separately in high- and low-VIX regimes.

The full production design combines CRSP monthly stock returns with Compustat accounting information through the CRSP/Compustat Merged database and uses size, book-to-market, and momentum as the core predictors. The current stage of the research establishes and validates the empirical forecasting pipeline on real WRDS data before expanding to the full production panel. The preliminary implementation uses size and momentum only and deliberately does not fabricate book-to-market before the accounting-data merge is complete.

The first real-data validation focuses on AAPL as a displayed stock while retaining cross-sectional model training. This distinction is important: the models are not trained on AAPL alone. Instead, they are estimated on a historical cross-section of stocks and the AAPL predictions are extracted from the resulting out-of-sample prediction panel. The preliminary findings show that the pipeline is operational and leakage-aware, while also demonstrating that strong single-stock time-series predictive performance is not mechanically produced by the methodology. The next empirical stage evaluates whether the same model predictions contain stronger cross-sectional ranking information through long-short portfolio sorts.
