# Conditional Research Contribution — Three Versions

## A. Concise (approximately 120 words)
This study evaluates whether historically observed market-volatility conditions are associated with differences in the statistical and economic value of cross-sectional U.S. stock-return forecasts. It compares a regularized linear model, Elastic Net, with a nonlinear boosted-tree model, XGBoost, using the same three stock characteristics, a strictly expanding historical estimation design, and time-available VIX state labels. Rather than treating small gains in forecast fit as investment profitability, the framework separately analyzes monthly ranking correlations, formal state contrasts, equal- and value-weighted long–short returns, portfolio turnover, and modeled transaction costs. Initial results suggest a descriptive difference between volatility states, but adjusted direct regime tests and value-weighted performance materially qualify the signal. The paper's contribution will depend on whether these contrasts withstand strong benchmarks, alternative state definitions, investability screens and temporal robustness analysis.

## B. Detailed introduction draft (approximately 350 words)
A central difficulty in empirical asset pricing is that forecasting individual-stock returns and earning implementable portfolio returns are distinct tasks. Modern machine-learning methods can identify nonlinear combinations of characteristics, but evidence of predictive rankings does not automatically establish conditional differences across market states or positive net investment returns. [Gu, Kelly and Xiu (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhaa009) show that flexible machine-learning specifications can improve expected-return estimation, while [Freyberger, Neuhierl and Weber (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhz123) document the importance of nonlinear characteristic relations. The role of market volatility is also economically salient. Yet related evidence, particularly [Avramov, Cheng and Metzker (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4449), cautions that machine-learning strategy profits may depend on difficult-to-arbitrage stocks and attenuate in high-volatility conditions or after trading frictions. Consequently, neither machine learning itself nor volatility-dependent profitability can be presented as a new discovery. [Jensen and coauthors (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhag022) further establish cost-aware ML portfolio optimization, limiting any claim that modeling turnover costs is itself new.

The present study asks a more restricted question: how does the *measured* return forecasting and investment value of one identical information set vary across states defined using market information available at portfolio formation? The design uses the monthly US CRSP–Compustat universe, size, book-to-market and momentum 12–2; forecasts next-month returns with Elastic Net and XGBoost under a fixed historical model-selection protocol and monthly expanding-window refits; and partitions the already-generated forecasts using expanding-median and expanding-tercile VIX state definitions. Each model is evaluated through a hierarchy of outcomes: stock-level forecast fit, monthly Spearman rank IC, equal- and value-weighted predicted-return decile spreads, HAC inference, direct state-difference tests, and an explicit turnover-based 0/50-bp cost model. This hierarchy is designed to prevent a favorable within-state coefficient from being misread as evidence of a significant state contrast.

The existing data show a positive LOW-state equal-weighted nonlinear signal under stylized trading costs, but the performance does not persist under value weighting, and direct three-state contrasts do not survive the specified Holm adjustment. These are limitations rather than inconvenient exceptions: they raise questions about microcaps, liquidity, time stability and alternate explanations. Subsequent stages therefore compare additional non-ML benchmarks, broaden characteristics, vary state boundaries, impose implementability filters, test subperiod stability and examine factor exposures. If the signal weakens, a transparent negative result can still identify which conditional predictive patterns fail to withstand realistic controls; if it persists, the paper can offer evidence about the economically relevant conditions of nonlinear predictability. Either interpretation remains observational, and the eventual contribution must be judged against the strongest existing volatility and machine-learning asset-pricing studies.

## C. Faculty advisor discussion summary
1. **Question:** conditional *cross-sectional* predictability, not aggregate market prediction or a trading-strategy launch.
2. **Novelty constraint:** Avramov et al. already connect volatility, ML, microcaps and costs; precise state-contrast inference and controlled benchmarks must substantiate differentiation.
3. **Existing results:** LOW XGBoost EW net positive, VW weak/negative; direct state contrasts not Holm-significant.
4. **Next evidence:** benchmarks (M12), predictor robustness (M13), VIX definitions (M14), microcap/cost realism (M15), subperiod stability (M16), mechanisms (M17).
5. **Integrity:** observations and hypotheses postdate an initial results inspection; avoid preregistration, causal or first-paper claims.
